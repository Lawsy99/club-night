## Decisions so far

- **Names:** Club Night, Wexley Chess Club, the Red Lion, Kingsbridge, Castlebury are fine for now.
- **Toby on trial night:** the last game of the night, "just for fun", at full engine strength with no help, so you lose. He shows as "unrated" and it doesn't count. From Act 1 onwards his rating is shown and scaled like everyone else's. *(Built.)*
- **Your name:** Graham takes it for the membership list on trial night. Characters use it. You never speak otherwise. *(Built.)*
- **Toby at the top of the ladder:** yes. He is genuinely very good and fake-humble about it.
- **The Act 2 reveal:** Toby sends you the wrong Lichess study, "Prep: [your name]", full of Pemberton's notes. The notes are about your real weaknesses, taken from your own games, so the study is genuinely useful to you as well as a betrayal.
- **Saving the club:** Dex's stream brings in new members. Dex is a quiet, reluctant friend; he never admits he did it on purpose.
- **Favouritism in Act 1:** obvious by the end of the act, but never stated. No single moment gives it away.
- **The very end:** a real last-round game against Toby at the final congress, followed by his excuses.

## How the story is told

The aim is simple, sophisticated and clean: something that draws you back, which a game on Lichess doesn't.

- **Understatement.** Say less than you mean. The player works out what's going on; nobody explains it.
- **Plain observation.** Stage directions describe only what you could see across the table: "(Clive pours from his flask.)" Never what it means, and never a punchline.
- **No narrator.** No "somehow", no "which is worse", no winks at the player.
- **Character, not jokes.** The humour comes from people being exactly themselves, played straight.
- **Small and specific.** A tea urn, a scoresheet, a clock on the wall. Real club life, not comedy club life.
- **Short.** One line per moment. Most games have two: one before, one after.

## What keeps you coming back

- **Every chapter ends on something unresolved.** It's a small open question, not a cliffhanger: a name on the honours board, a team sheet in pencil, a phone that was recording.
- **The club changes where you can see it.** The noticeboard, the empty second room filling up, your name going on the honours board.
- **The rivalry keeps score.** Your head-to-head record with Toby is always on the Home screen, and he always seems slightly ahead.
- **Your chess is part of the story.** The scouting reports and Toby's study are built from your actual games, so the story is about how you actually play.

## Act 1 in detail: "Club nights"

Trial night, seven chapters (one per club regular), then the knockout cup. Each chapter is roughly one week. The story happens only in lines before and after games, and on the noticeboard, never as cutscenes.

### Trial night

- Marjorie introduces you around. Graham takes your name for the membership list. Pemberton watches from the side.
- Four placement games: Marjorie, Dex, Graham, Clive. Pemberton tells you your rating.
- Toby, also new, asks for a quick game before the chairs go up. He wins easily. "Beginner's luck, honestly."
- Pemberton watched Toby's game. He didn't watch yours.
- **Open question:** the honours board. One name, V. Hart **[?]**, is club champion year after year, then stops.

### Chapter 1: Marjorie

- She makes the tea and plays the London System. She's kind, and she beats you in an endgame you thought was a draw.
- **Open question:** "We used to need both rooms."

### Chapter 2: Dex

- He plays gambits with his phone propped against a water bottle. "It's not recording. Probably."
- After you beat him: "Don't clip that."
- **Open question:** the phone was recording.

### Chapter 3: Oscar

- He plays quickly and says almost nothing. Neil, his dad, talks for both of them.
- **Open question:** Neil asks Pemberton to coach Oscar. Pemberton says his Tuesdays are taken. (Toby's are the Tuesdays.)

### Chapter 4: Clive

- He offers a draw at move twelve, every game. Beating him means making something out of nothing.
- **Open question:** he mentions he played board one "when there was a proper board one", then talks about the weather.

### Chapter 5: Priya

- Twenty moves of theory, then twenty minutes on one move.
- **Open question:** after you beat her, quietly: "What do you do when the book runs out?"

### Chapter 6: Graham

- The pub wants eight pounds more a night. Graham has done a spreadsheet.
- **Open question:** a draft team sheet goes up, in pencil. Toby is on board two after four weeks.

### Chapter 7: Toby

- Your first real game since trial night. He's on the membership list now, with a rating like everyone else.
- He offers to go over games together and sends you his Lichess study.
- Pemberton's scouting report on him is two lines long. Everyone else's was a page.

### The knockout cup

- Three rounds (Oscar, Clive, Graham), then the final against Toby. Marjorie gets out the good set.
- **If you lose:** "Good game! Want to go over it?" Qualify again next month.
- **If you win:** "So deserved. Genuinely." Pemberton congratulates you, then goes to find Toby.
- **The act ends on:** a new sheet on the noticeboard, "Club ladder", with Toby's name already at the top.

## For review

### A. Which character is the Taylor Doose figure? (proposal)

**Recommendation:** Graham. He already runs the rules and the money, so the bureaucrat is a small step. He becomes the club's self-appointed authority on everything:
- general meetings and sub-committees;
- laminated notices, and a newsletter nobody asked for;
- a long-running correspondence with the brewery about the room.

He stays precise, pompous and never unkind. It also gives the noticeboard an owner: most notices are his, in his voice. Sample lines (new, in the spirit of the character):

- "I've called an extraordinary general meeting. It's on the noticeboard. Laminated."
- "Under rule fourteen, phones are to be off. Not silent. Off."
- "The centenary committee met on Tuesday. I am the centenary committee."
- "I've written to the brewery again. Recorded delivery."

The alternative is a new character, for example the pub landlord or the league secretary. That adds someone to the cast rather than sharpening someone already in it.
